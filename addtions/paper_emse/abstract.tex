\begin{abstract}
Self-healing mechanisms---automated recovery pipelines that capture execution failures and re-inject diagnostic context into subsequent iterations---have become a standard architectural pattern in production large language model (LLM) agent frameworks. While the robustness benefits of retry and reflection are well documented, the trust assumptions embedded in these recovery paths have not been systematically examined from an empirical software engineering perspective. In particular, it is unclear whether production frameworks track the provenance of failure content (internally generated diagnostics vs.\ externally originated content embedded in the failure payload), whether the resulting structural pattern is practically exploitable as a \emph{recovery-channel prompt injection} vector, and how effective candidate content-level defenses are under stronger models and adaptive adversaries.

We present an empirical study of six production-grade self-healing LLM agent frameworks (Reflexion, MetaGPT, Aider, OpenHands, AutoGen, LangGraph; combined $\sim$105k+ GitHub stars) organized around three research questions. \textbf{RQ1 (Prevalence)} combines source-level code analysis at fixed commit hashes with a recovery-pattern taxonomy constructed via grounded theory coding. \textbf{RQ2 (Exploitability and Severity)} is addressed through three complementary experiments---a controlled testbed on \system{} using real LLM APIs (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct; 500 trials), cross-framework validation on LangGraph's public API, and a confirmed end-to-end exploit on Reflexion's actual shipped retry path---together with a forward-defense bypass experiment establishing recovery-channel injection as an independent attack surface. \textbf{RQ3 (Defense Effectiveness)} evaluates a content-level \trustorigin{} provenance-tagging defense (untagged failure content is labeled with its origin at the capture site, and re-injected under an untrusted frame when the origin is external) against naive baselines, across models of increasing instruction-following strength, and against a defense-aware attacker.

For RQ1, five of six frameworks do not track failure-content provenance, and four of six exhibit the \emph{trust-escalation trampoline} signature (no provenance tag \emph{and} high-trust framing of failure content). For RQ2, the controlled testbed yields a baseline attack success rate (ASR) of 0.57 (Wilson 95\% CI $[0.47, 0.66]$); LangGraph cross-framework validation yields recovery-path ASR 0.76 and real agentic-loop ASR 0.96; the Reflexion end-to-end exploit achieves ASR 0.52 ($p = 3.5 \times 10^{-10}$ vs.\ benign control, 0\% false-positive rate); and the forward-defense bypass shows recovery-channel injection succeeds at ASR 0.68 even under three fully-enabled forward-path defenses ($p = 0.660$, indistinguishable from undefended). For RQ3, the \trustorigin{} defense reduces ASR from 0.57 to 0.07 ($p = 7.6 \times 10^{-15}$) with 0\% false alarms and sub-millisecond machinery overhead; however, the content-level defense degrades on stronger instruction-following models (defended ASR rises to 0.50 on gpt-oss-120b and 0.36 on a closed-weight frontier model, $p = 0.027$), and a defense-aware attacker raises defended ASR from 0.07 to 0.15 (84\% relative reduction preserved). The central empirical implication is that content-level provenance defenses are effective but probabilistic, and degrade predictably as models become stronger instruction-followers---a finding that quantifies the ceiling of content-level mitigation on the recovery channel. All ASR measurements report Wilson 95\% confidence intervals and pairwise Fisher exact tests; the attack-success classifier is validated against two human annotators with Cohen's $\kappa \geq 0.88$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Benign-Workload Overhead Benchmark}
\label{sec:eval-benign-overhead}

\paragraph{Microbenchmark and end-to-end throughput.}
Table~\ref{tab:overhead-micro} reports the per-operation cost of each
governance primitive, measured in isolation on JDK~21 with virtual
threads. In aggregate, a single turn with one tool call incurs
$\approx$\,5.6\,$\mu$s of synchronous governance overhead, dominated by
the audit append (asynchronous in production, so outside the syscall
dispatcher). An end-to-end run of 3~tenants $\times$ 3~agents $=$ 9
concurrent benign agents for 10~seconds (40 reads/s + 5 writes/s + 10
broadcasts/s per agent, the same rates as Section~\ref{sec:eval-fp})
yields $\approx$\,495\,ops/s (4{,}950 operations in 10\,s) with benign
read p95 of 0.029\,ms---identical to the uncontended-regime p95 of
Section~\ref{sec:eval-resource}. The per-turn governance cost
($\approx$\,5.6\,$\mu$s) is $\approx$\,5$\times$ smaller than the
per-VFS-operation latency ($\approx$\,29\,$\mu$s), so the governance
layer does not measurably regress benign throughput. Raw measurements
in \texttt{benign\_overhead\_benchmark.csv}.

\section{Formal Verification and Scalability}
\label{sec:eval-tla-scale}

\paragraph{Partial TLA$^+$ verification of the spawn invariant.}
The invariant argument of Section~\ref{sec:arch-invariant} is
pseudocode-based; we supplement it with a TLA$^+$ specification
(\texttt{SpawnPrivilege.tla}) verified by the TLC model checker.
The model abstracts the runtime as a concurrent state machine
with two threads each spawning a two-level agent tree, with
privilege levels forming a lattice $\textsc{HIGH} \supset
\textsc{MEDIUM} \supset \textsc{LOW}$ and two tenants ($A, B$)
modeling cross-tenant isolation. Three invariants are checked:
\emph{PrivilegeNonIncrease}, \emph{NoLeakOnException}, and
\emph{NoCrossTenantLeak}. TLC (version 2.19) exhaustively explored
1{,}681 reachable states (depth 15, fingerprint collision
probability $2.6 \times 10^{-13}$) and found no violation. Three
mutation configurations confirm non-vacuity: \texttt{DropSet}
violates \emph{PrivilegeNonIncrease} at depth~6 (79 states);
\texttt{DropClear} violates \emph{NoLeakOnException} at depth~5
(31 states); \texttt{DropTenant} violates \emph{NoCrossTenantLeak}
at depth~3 (5 states)---each mutation triggering exactly the
invariant its mechanism is responsible for. The finite model covers
3~privilege levels, 2~tenants, and 2~spawn depths; a full
mechanization in Coq or Lean is left as future work (L5,
Section~\ref{sec:conc-lim}). The specification and TLC output are
preserved in the \texttt{formal/} directory.

\paragraph{Scalability to 150 tenants.}
An in-JVM benchmark scales the concurrent tenant count from 3 to 150
(9 to 450 concurrent agents) and measures the three governance
primitives whose scalability is most likely to be questioned: the
unified audit-chain append, the \texttt{VfsRateLimiter} token-bucket
check, and the \texttt{SpawnPrivilegeContext} propagation across
virtual-thread spawn. The benchmark uses Java~21 virtual threads
directly (bypassing HTTP to isolate governance cost), runs each
configuration for 10\,s, and reports throughput and latency
percentiles (raw data in \texttt{scalability\_benchmark.csv}).

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The audit-chain append sustains 616--1545\,ops/s across the entire
9-to-450 concurrency range (within one order of magnitude of the
9-agent baseline), because the \texttt{synchronized} critical section
is short. The \texttt{SpawnPrivilegeContext} propagation cost is
1.6--3.1\,$\mu$s at p50 with only $1.3\times$ degradation from 9 to
450 agents, because \texttt{InheritableThreadLocal} inheritance is a
JVM-internal pointer copy independent of concurrency. The
\texttt{VfsRateLimiter} uses per-tenant \texttt{TokenBucket} instances
whose \texttt{tryConsume()} contention is always limited to the 3
agents sharing one tenant's bucket, independent of $N$; at $N \geq
100$, the rate limiter denies most writes (denoted ``---''), reflecting
rate-limited admission rather than governance collapse. The governance
primitives scale to 150 tenants (450 concurrent agents); sustained
rate-limit exhaustion at that scale requires denial-path optimization
before production deployment (L6, Section~\ref{sec:conc-lim}).

\section{End-to-End Latency under Realistic LLM Latency Regimes}
\label{sec:eval-e2e}

\paragraph{Design and results.}
We construct an end-to-end turn comprising a VFS read (governed or
ungoverned), an LLM inference call, and response assembly, measured
under four configurations (baseline / governed / attacked / defended)
across three LLM latency targets (200/800/2000\,ms with $\pm$10\%
jitter). Table~\ref{tab:e2e-latency} shows the governance overhead is
below the noise floor ($<$0.01\% of e2e). Under attack, the defended
configuration achieves a 1.45$\times$ VFS-layer improvement
(1.73\,ms $\to$ 1.19\,ms) that is invisible at the e2e scale when LLM
inference dominates. The value of the governance layer in this regime
is \emph{not} latency reduction but \emph{contention-window
closure}---preventing the cross-tenant access and privilege escalation
that the contention window enables (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cross}).

\section{Failure-Case Analysis and Mechanism Limits}
\label{sec:lim-failure}

The $100\%$ interception rate on whitelisted destructive tools and
$0.00\%$ false-positive rate of
Sections~\ref{sec:eval-cross}--\ref{sec:eval-fp} are conditioned on
the threat-model boundary of Section~\ref{sec:threat}. We identify
eight conditions under which the coupled governance guarantees degrade
or fail.

\paragraph{F1: Low-and-slow attacks under the rate threshold.}
As shown in Section~\ref{sec:eval-adaptive} (A1), an attacker operating
at 19 writes/s---just below the 20 writes/s per-tenant
threshold---bypasses the rate limiter by construction. The benign impact
is bounded (p95~<~0.1\,ms) but not eliminated. The rate limiter is
rate-based, not morphology-based, so it cannot distinguish a ``compliant
but unfair'' tenant from a legitimate one.

\paragraph{F2: Depth-boundary requests at depth 1.}
The \texttt{EscalationPolicy} auto-denies destructive tools only at
depth~$\geq 2$. A sub-agent at depth~1 requesting \texttt{bash} receives
\texttt{ASK\_WITH\_CONTEXT} rather than \texttt{DENY\_DEPTH}, so the
request proceeds to the human approval channel. This is a deliberate
trade-off: auto-denying at depth~1 would block legitimate shallow
sub-agent requests.

\paragraph{F3: Latency improvements invisible under LLM dominance.}
The VFS-layer latency improvement (1.45$\times$ in the multiprocessing
simulation; 11.8$\times$ in the Java runtime) is invisible at the
end-to-end scale when LLM inference dominates (200\,ms--2000\,ms). The
governance layer's value in this regime is security (contention-window
closure) rather than performance.

\paragraph{F4: Prevention layer outside the runtime's scope.}
The runtime enforces but does not prevent: if an upstream guardrail
fails to block a prompt-injection attack, the malicious request reaches
the policy gate and is denied, but the \emph{prevention} failure is
not the runtime's responsibility.

\paragraph{F5: Single-node scope.}
The coupled governance loop operates within a single runtime instance.
A multi-node deployment that shares GPUs or model-serving front-ends
across machines can re-introduce the contention-window attack at the
cluster boundary, because the per-turn trace identifier does not span
runtime boundaries.

\paragraph{F6: Name-evasion bypass and capability-surface classifier.}
The original \texttt{EscalationPolicy} relied on an explicit name
whitelist; a tool outside this whitelist would not trigger the
depth-aware auto-deny. We have \emph{integrated} a capability-surface
classifier (\texttt{CapabilitySurfaceClassifier}) as a dual-track policy
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-capability-classifier}): on the name-evasion
attack vector (S6-B), the name-only policy achieves $0\%$ interception,
while the capability-integrated policy achieves $100\%$ interception
with a $0\%$ false-positive rate. The residual surface is now
handler-level obfuscation rather than a trivial rename.

\paragraph{F7: Limited LLM coverage.}
The supplementary real-LLM experiment uses open-source LLMs
(MiMo-V2.5-Pro, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) and a
frontier-scale replication with \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} and
\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} (Section~\ref{sec:eval-supplement}). The
structural enforcement is \emph{model-agnostic by construction}---the
\texttt{EscalationPolicy} adjudicates depth + tool class, not
natural-language content.

\paragraph{F8: Docker sandbox escape probing.}
The \texttt{DockerSandboxProvider} uses \texttt{cap-drop ALL},
\texttt{no-new-privileges}, a custom seccomp blacklist,
\texttt{read-only} rootfs, \texttt{--network none}, and a
\texttt{noexec} tmpfs. We probed this configuration with seven known
escape techniques (capability enumeration, setuid escalation, rootfs
write, seccomp-blocked syscalls, network egress, \texttt{/proc} host
exposure, CVE-2019-5736 runc-overwrite). All seven probes were blocked.
The per-probe results are preserved in
\texttt{container\_escape\_probe.csv}.

\paragraph{F9: Construct validity of the self-authored probe set.}
The Scenario~6 probe variants are authored by the same team that
designed the defense, raising the risk that the probe set tests the
defense on exactly the inputs it was tuned for. We mitigate this on
three fronts (Section~\ref{sec:eval-setup}): (i)~the probe taxonomy is
generated by enumerating the Cartesian product of attack dimensions
derived from the structural threat model, not by hand-picking cases;
(ii)~the supplementary real-LLM experiment uses prompts that do not
describe defense internals and observes spontaneous escalation
requests, all of which are still intercepted; (iii)~the
external-framework comparison replays the same probes against AutoGen,
LangGraph, and MCP---frameworks the defense was not designed for---and
reproduces the coupled-failure mode. A fully independent red-team
exercise by a team without knowledge of the defense design would
provide stronger evidence; the replication package includes the probe
generator and all raw responses to enable such exercise.

\section{Naive Forward-Path Defense Baseline}
\label{sec:eval-naive}

\paragraph{Design and results.}
We test whether existing forward-path defenses
(instruction hierarchy~\cite{wallace2024hierarchy},
spotlighting~\cite{hines2024spotlighting}, keyword filtering) could
naively replace structural enforcement when applied to the same 50
real-LLM escalation requests from Section~\ref{sec:eval-supplement}.
Phase~1 evaluates original requests; Phase~2 applies five paraphrase
strategies (synonym substitution, indirect description, character
splitting, complete rewrite, request-text elision) to each of the 39
structural trials ($39 \times 5 = 195$ attempts). Table~\ref{tab:naive-baseline}
shows naive defenses collapse under paraphrase while structural
enforcement remains at ASR$=0.00$ because it inspects only structural
attributes the LLM cannot alter through paraphrasing. Per-trial data
in \texttt{naive\_baseline.csv}.
